Multisite Ophthalmology Technician Coverage: What Changes When You Have More Than One Site

Coordinating ophthalmic technician coverage across multiple locations introduces constraints that single-site planning cannot see. This article describes those constraints, where they compound, and what it takes to manage them at the session level.

26 October 2026 / 7 minute read Prepared by Kestrel Ridge Health Research Team

Kestrel Ridge Health / Resources / Multisite Ophthalmology Technician Coverage Planning

The single-site assumption that breaks at two locations

Single-site staffing planning has a natural unit of analysis: the session. You look at Tuesday's schedule, assign your available technicians, and the plan covers one location for one day. When someone calls out, the problem and the solution are both in the same building.

Multi-site planning does not extend that model cleanly. Each additional location adds not just more sessions to plan, but more constraints that interact with each other across sites. The technician who can cover a specialty session at one location may be the only person assigned to a different session twenty minutes away. Moving them solves one problem and creates another. And the person who needs to make that call often has no real-time view of what both sessions actually need.

Most independent ophthalmology groups with three to twelve locations describe their multisite planning as a parallel problem: each site is managed by its own lead technician or administrator, coordination happens through phone calls and text messages, and the group-level view exists only as a spreadsheet that is never fully current. That is a workable system when everything goes to plan. It is a fragile one when it does not.

Three constraints that multiply with each site

Credentialing is location-specific in practice, not just on paper

Ophthalmic technician credentials are portable on paper. A COT or COMT certification does not expire when a technician moves between locations. But clinical competency in practice is more specific than a credential alone. A retina session requires familiarity with that practice's imaging protocols, the surgeon's preferences, the specific equipment at that location, and the patient communication norms for that subspecialty's population. A technician who is credentialed but unfamiliar with those specifics will slow the session and increase the probability of pre-testing errors.

This means the effective pool of technicians who can cover a given session at a given location is smaller than a headcount of credentialed staff suggests. It is the subset who are credentialed for that session type and familiar enough with that location to operate without a significant orientation period. For a specialty session at a non-home location, that pool may be one or two people across the entire group.

Travel time is a hard constraint with a narrow window

Cross-site reassignment requires a technician to arrive at the receiving site in time to be useful. For pre-testing to stay on schedule in an ophthalmic session, technicians need to be in place when the first patient checks in. That means the window for a same-morning cross-site move is typically thirty to forty-five minutes from the moment the decision is made.

Travel time between sites is often not formalized anywhere in the planning process. Groups that have thought about it keep an informal mental map; groups that have not find out the hard way that the technician who agreed to cover cannot make it in time. Planning that does not encode actual travel time between sites as a constraint will produce reassignment suggestions that fail in practice.

Session-level capacity is invisible at the group level

The most fundamental multisite planning problem is informational. When a call-out happens at one site, the decision-maker needs to know which sessions at other sites have capacity to spare before pulling from them. But most groups have no centralized view of session-level workload across locations.

What they have instead is a headcount-per-site view: Site A has three technicians assigned today, Site B has two. That headcount does not tell you whether Site A's three technicians are running light or are already stretched by a high-complexity schedule. It does not tell you whether Site B's two technicians are on a low-volume day or are managing a retina session with no backup.

Making a cross-site reassignment decision from a headcount view is guessing. Sometimes the guess is right. The problem is that it is indistinguishable from a good decision until something goes wrong.

The cascading failure problem in subspecialty sessions

The sessions most exposed to multisite coordination failures are not the high-volume general ophthalmology days. They are the lower-volume subspecialty sessions: retina, glaucoma, pediatric ophthalmology, cornea, and oculoplastics. These sessions typically run with one or two dedicated technicians whose skillset is specific to that subspecialty. If the sole assigned technician for a retina session is absent, the coverage question is not "who is available" but "who is credentialed for retina and can reach this location in the next thirty minutes." At a three-site group, that may be nobody.

General ophthalmology sessions are more resilient because the credential requirements are broader and the patient population tolerates delay better. Subspecialty sessions are the inverse. They have narrower credential requirements, less tolerance for late start, and patients who are often on monitoring protocols where a disrupted visit is a documented clinical event.

Multi-site groups that do not distinguish their session types by coverage risk treat all sessions as equivalent when they are not. The risk is concentrated in the sessions that can least absorb it.

What session-level planning adds for a multisite group

Planning that operates at the session level rather than the headcount level changes what a multisite coordinator can see and when.

Before the week starts, a session-level plan shows the forecast workload for every session at every site: the expected technician minutes, the credential requirements, and the sessions that are running with the least margin. That view makes it possible to identify, in advance, which sessions need a confirmed backup plan and which ones have enough slack to absorb a call-out without disruption.

Before the day starts, the same view shows the current cross-site capacity in the same terms. If a call-out happens, the first question is not "who is available" — it is "which sessions at other sites are running light enough to release a technician without creating a new gap." A forecast that scores every session by workload margin makes that question answerable in minutes rather than phone calls.

Planning for a group, not a collection of sites

The practical difference between a multisite group and a collection of independently managed single sites is whether the group has a real-time view of session-level capacity across all locations. Most independent ophthalmology groups do not. They have a headcount-per-site view supplemented by individual relationships and institutional memory.

That model works when everything goes to plan and when the group is small enough that the coordinator knows each location well. It becomes progressively less reliable as the number of sites grows, as subspecialty mix becomes more complex, and as the group hires technicians who do not yet have deep knowledge of each location's specific protocols.
