pb-10 pt-16 sm:pb-14 sm:pt-24" data-reveal style="opacity:1;transform:none"><h2 class="max-w-3xl font-serif text-2xl leading-[1.14] sm:text-[2rem]">The unit of analysis is the whole argument</h2><div class="mt-6"><div class="panel-grid surface-lead mt-6 grid gap-px sm:grid-cols-2"><div class="bg-card px-5 py-8 sm:px-7 sm:py-10"><p class="font-serif text-xl leading-snug text-ink sm:text-[1.375rem]">Ratio staffing asks: how many technicians should this practice employ?</p></div><div class="bg-card px-5 py-8 sm:px-7 sm:py-10"><p class="font-serif text-xl leading-snug text-ink sm:text-[1.375rem]">Workload forecasting asks: how many technician minutes will this session consume?</p></div></div><p class="prose-measure mt-4 text-base text-ink-muted">These are not competing answers to one question. They are answers to two different questions, and a practice needs both. The budget question is annual and financial. The staffing question is daily and operational. Using the annual answer to settle the daily question is where the error comes from.</p><p class="prose-measure mt-4 text-base text-ink-muted">Consider the concrete case. Two sessions, ten patients each, same provider, same room count. Session A is postoperative cataract follow-up: short histories, refractions, pressures, brief exams. Session B is new and complex glaucoma: full histories, visual fields, OCT, pachymetry, gonioscopy prep, longer dilations, more counselling. A ratio sees two identical ten-patient sessions and staffs them identically. Anyone who has run a clinic knows they are not identical.</p><ol class="surface-lead mt-6 px-5 sm:px-7 md:px-8"><li class="py-8 sm:py-10 "><h3 class="max-w-[20ch] font-mono text-[1.4375rem] font-medium leading-[1.18] tracking-[-0.025em] tabular-nums text-ink sm:text-[1.75rem]">354 and 198 technician minutes</h3><div class="mt-1"><p class="prose-measure mt-4 text-base text-ink-muted">Kestrel Ridge Health’s own model, measured on the system’s synthetic validation set and not validated on real clinic data, produces exactly this kind of split: two ten-patient sessions forecast at 354 and 198 technician minutes respectively. That figure is an illustration of what a forecast does with mix, not evidence about any real clinic.</p></div></li></ol></div></section><section id="literature" class="section-edge rule-top pb-10 pt-16 sm:pb-14 sm:pt-24" data-reveal style="opacity:1;transform:none"><h2 class="max-w-3xl font-serif text-2xl leading-[1.14] sm:text-[2rem]">What the ophthalmology literature actually establishes</h2><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-base text-ink-muted"><p>The strongest published evidence on this general approach is Hribar et al.,<!-- --> <a href="https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6391189/" class="text-primary underline underline-offset-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">“Data Driven Scheduling for Improving Patient Efficiency in Ophthalmology Clinics,” Ophthalmology 2019;126(3):347-354</a>.</p></div><div class="mt-10 sm:mt-12"><p class="prose-measure mt-4 text-base text-ink-muted">The team built a discrete event simulation from electronic health record timestamp data, validated the simulation against 120 directly observed appointments, and used it to design a scheduling template that sorted patients into short, medium, and long slots based on diagnosis and provider notes. Their validation results are worth reading carefully:</p><ul class="surface mt-6 space-y-3 py-6 pl-10 pr-6 text-base leading-relaxed text-ink-muted [&amp;&gt;li]:max-w-[68ch] list-disc"><li>EHR-derived physician exam time was 13.8 plus or minus 8.2 minutes against directly observed 13.3 plus or minus 7.3 minutes, p = 0.7.</li><li>Simulated wait time was 31.2 plus or minus 10.9 minutes against observed 32.6 plus or minus 25.3 minutes, p = 0.9.</li><li>After implementation across five providers, all five showed statistically significant volume increases of 1 to 3 patients per session, and four of five achieved 3 to 4 minute average wait time reductions.</li></ul><h3 class="mt-10 font-serif text-lg leading-snug text-ink sm:text-xl">What this paper does prove</h3><p class="prose-measure mt-4 text-base text-ink-muted">That routinely captured EHR timestamp data is an accurate enough record of clinic durations to model against, and that a duration-aware template outperforms a uniform one. That is the load-bearing premise underneath any workload forecast: the durations are predictable from data you already have.</p><h3 class="mt-10 font-serif text-lg leading-snug text-ink sm:text-xl">What this paper does not prove</h3><p class="prose-measure mt-4 text-base text-ink-muted">Hribar’s team predicted physician exam time and patient wait time. They did not forecast technician minutes, and they did not build a staffing assignment model. Their unit of change was the scheduling template, not the technician roster. Anyone who tells you this paper validates technician-workload forecasting is overreading it. It validates the premise. The application to technician staffing is an extension that has not, as far as public literature shows, been demonstrated at scale.</p><p class="prose-measure mt-4 text-base text-ink-muted">The methodological case for volume-driven rather than ratio-driven staffing in ophthalmology specifically was made by Maureen Waddle in<!-- --> <a href="https://ophthalmologymanagement.com/issues/2011/april/do-i-have-the-right-number-of-staff/" class="text-primary underline underline-offset-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Ophthalmology Management, April 2011</a>, which proposes modelling staff need from three work units, office visits, diagnostic tests, and surgeries, rather than from provider headcount. That is a forecast in everything but name, built from a time-flow study rather than from machine learning. The idea is not new. What has changed is that the data needed to fit it is now sitting in every practice management system.</p></div></section><section id="inputs" class="section-edge rule-top pb-10 pt-16 sm:pb-14 sm:pt-24" data-reveal style="opacity:1;transform:none"><h2 class="max-w-3xl font-serif text-2xl leading-[1.14] sm:text-[2rem]">What a session-level forecast needs as input</h2><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-base text-ink-muted"><p>A workload forecast is only as good as the operational record behind it. In practice the minimum is:</p></div><div class="mt-10 sm:mt-12"><div class="panel-grid surface mt-6 grid gap-px sm:grid-cols-2"><div class="bg-card px-5 py-6 sm:px-6 sm:py-7 "><h3 class="font-serif text-base leading-snug text-ink sm:text-lg">Per-session appointment mix</h3><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>Counts by appointment type for each historical session. Not just a patient count. New patient, established follow-up, postoperative day one, postoperative week one, injection visit, and so on, as your own schedule types are actually coded.</p></div></div><div class="bg-card px-5 py-6 sm:px-6 sm:py-7 "><h3 class="font-serif text-base leading-snug text-ink sm:text-lg">Diagnostic testing load</h3><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>Which tests were performed, per session. Visual fields, OCT, fundus photography, topography, biometry, pachymetry. This is where a large share of technician minutes goes, and it is the variable that most sharply separates two same-count sessions.</p></div></div><div class="bg-card px-5 py-6 sm:px-6 sm:py-7 "><h3 class="font-serif text-base leading-snug text-ink sm:text-lg">Complexity flags</h3><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>Whatever your practice already records that predicts a longer workup. Dilation requirements, interpreter need, mobility assistance, first-visit paperwork burden, subspecialty designation. These do not need to be new fields. Most practices already capture proxies.</p></div></div><div class="bg-card px-5 py-6 sm:px-6 sm:py-7 "><h3 class="font-serif text-base leading-snug text-ink sm:text-lg">Realized technician minutes</h3><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>The dependent variable. This is the input most practices are missing, and it is the honest gate on this entire approach. If your system does not record when a technician started and finished with a patient, a forecast has nothing to learn from. Some practice management and EHR systems timestamp workup start and end; some do not. Establishing whether yours does is the first practical question, ahead of any vendor conversation.</p></div></div><div class="bg-card px-5 py-6 sm:px-6 sm:py-7 sm:col-span-2"><h3 class="font-serif text-base leading-snug text-ink sm:text-lg">What it does not need</h3><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>It does not need patient identifiers. Appointment type counts, test counts, complexity flags, and durations are operational data. A forecast of technician minutes has no use for a name, a date of birth, or a diagnosis code attached to a person. Any vendor telling you they need identified patient records to forecast staffing minutes should be asked to justify it specifically.</p></div></div></div></div></section><section id="measuring-quality" class="section-edge rule-top pb-10 pt-16 sm:pb-14 sm:pt-24" data-reveal style="opacity:1;transform:none"><h2 class="max-w-3xl font-serif text-2xl leading-[1.14] sm:text-[2rem]">How forecast quality is measured, and how to read a vendor&#x27;s number</h2><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-base text-ink-muted"><p>The standard measure is mean absolute error: on average, how many minutes was the forecast off by, in either direction. It is the right metric here because it is in the units you actually manage, minutes, rather than a unitless score.</p></div><div class="mt-10 sm:mt-12"><p class="mt-9 max-w-[24ch] border-t-2 border-rule-strong pt-5 font-serif text-[1.625rem] leading-[1.16] text-ink sm:text-[2rem]">The comparison that matters is not forecast error against zero.</p><p class="prose-measure mt-4 text-base text-ink-muted">It is forecast error against what you do today. If your current practice is to staff every session from a fixed ratio, then the fixed ratio’s error on the same sessions is the number to beat, and a vendor who will not show you that comparison is showing you half a result.</p><div class="mt-8"><div class="surface-limit px-5 py-5 sm:px-6"><p class="font-serif text-base font-semibold text-ink">What a synthetic result can and cannot support</p><div class="prose-measure mt-2 text-[0.9375rem] text-ink-muted"><p>For illustration of what such a comparison looks like: Kestrel Ridge Health reports a mean absolute error of 23.76 minutes, against 24.01 for a linear regression with one coefficient per provider and 33.42 for a spreadsheet rule of patients times that provider’s average, all measured on the system’s synthetic validation set and none validated on real clinic data. A fixed technician-to-provider ratio scores 208.61 on the same sessions, a naive floor rather than the comparison that matters. Synthetic validation shows that the method runs and that the model is about level with a plain regression on data generated to resemble clinic operations. It does not show that either number will hold on a real schedule, and it should not be read as if it does. The same comparison run against a clinic’s own history is a different and much stronger claim, and it is the claim any practice should require before spending money.</p></div></div></div><p class="prose-measure mt-4 text-base text-ink-muted">Two further questions to ask of any forecast number:</p><ol class="surface mt-6 space-y-3 py-6 pl-10 pr-6 text-base leading-relaxed text-ink-muted [&amp;&gt;li]:max-w-[68ch] list-decimal"><li><strong class="font-semibold text-ink">Was it validated out of sample?</strong> <!-- -->A model scored on the data it was fit to will look better than it is. Ask whether the reported error is on held-out sessions.</li><li><strong class="font-semibold text-ink">What is the error distribution, not just the mean?</strong> <!-- -->A mean absolute error of 25 minutes made up of consistent small misses is a different operational reality from one made up of mostly perfect forecasts and occasional 3-hour misses. Ask for the tail.</li></ol></div></section><section id="failure-modes" class="section-edge rule-top pb-10 pt-16 sm:pb-14 sm:pt-24" data-reveal style="opacity:1;transform:none"><h2 class="max-w-3xl font-serif text-2xl leading-[1.14] sm:text-[2rem]">Where forecasting fails</h2><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-base text-ink-muted"><p>Being clear about failure modes is more useful than the sales case.</p></div><div class="mt-10 sm:mt-12"><div class="panel-grid surface mt-6 grid gap-px "><div class="bg-card px-5 py-6 sm:px-6 sm:py-7 "><p class="font-serif text-base font-semibold leading-snug text-ink sm:text-lg">Thin or dirty history.</p><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>A forecast learns from the past. A practice with six months of inconsistently coded appointment types has little to learn from. This is the most common real-world blocker.</p></div></div><div class="bg-card px-5 py-6 sm:px-6 sm:py-7 "><p class="font-serif text-base font-semibold leading-snug text-ink sm:text-lg">Regime change.</p><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>A new subspecialty service line, a new imaging device, a new EHR, or a departing high-volume provider all break the relationship the model learned. A forecast is a statement about a stable operation.</p></div></div><div class="bg-card px-5 py-6 sm:px-6 sm:py-7 "><p class="font-serif text-base font-semibold leading-snug text-ink sm:text-lg">Rare events.</p><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>Forecasts are poor at the sessions that hurt most, the unusual ones. They are best at the ordinary majority.</p></div></div><div class="bg-card px-5 py-6 sm:px-6 sm:py-7 "><p class="font-serif text-base font-semibold leading-snug text-ink sm:text-lg">No slack to allocate.</p><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>A forecast that correctly tells you Tuesday needs two more technicians is useless if you do not have two more technicians. Forecasting reallocates existing capacity and sizes a real gap. It does not create staff. In a market where <a href="https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/29-2057.00" class="text-primary underline underline-offset-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">O*NET reports</a> 78,800 ophthalmic medical technicians employed as of 2024 against roughly 12,500 projected openings over the 2024 to 2034 decade, the hiring constraint is real and forecasting does not relieve it.</p></div></div><div class="bg-card px-5 py-6 sm:px-6 sm:py-7 "><p class="font-serif text-base font-semibold leading-snug text-ink sm:text-lg">A forecast is not a decision.</p><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>A number is an input. Coverage, credentialing, fairness, and staff preference are judgment. The forecast should inform the person who makes the roster, not replace them.</p></div></div></div></div></section><section id="assignment" class="section-edge rule-top pb-10 pt-16 sm:pb-14 sm:pt-24" data-reveal style="opacity:1;transform:none"><h2 class="max-w-3xl font-serif text-2xl leading-[1.14] sm:text-[2rem]">Forecasting and assignment are separate problems</h2><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-base text-ink-muted"><p>One further distinction that is easy to collapse. Forecasting tells you how many technician minutes a session needs. Assignment tells you which technicians can supply them.</p></div><div class="mt-10 sm:mt-12"><p class="prose-measure mt-4 text-base text-ink-muted">The second problem is bounded by constraints the first knows nothing about: site privileges, credential scope as defined by the<!-- --> <a href="https://www.jcahpo.org/certification/" class="text-primary underline underline-offset-4" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">IJCAHPO</a> COA, COT, and COMT credentials and their specialty certifications, device competency sign-offs, hour caps, and callout coverage.</p><p class="prose-measure mt-4 text-base text-ink-muted">Those constraints are hard rules, not preferences, and any system operating on them should treat them as such: a plan that violates a credentialing rule should not be produced at all, rather than produced with a warning. Kestrel Ridge Health enforces eight such rules and requires a human to approve every plan before it takes effect; the mechanism is documented on<!-- --> <a class="text-primary underline underline-offset-4" href="/how-it-works/">how it works</a>, and the current validation status, including that the system runs on synthetic data and is not HIPAA certified, is stated on<!-- --> <a class="text-primary underline underline-offset-4" href="/evidence/">evidence</a>.</p></div></section><section id="first-step" class="section-edge rule-top pb-10 pt-16 sm:pb-14 sm:pt-24" data-reveal style="opacity:1;transform:none"><h2 class="max-w-3xl font-serif text-2xl leading-[1.14] sm:text-[2rem]">The realistic first step</h2><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-base text-ink-muted"><p>You do not need to buy anything to find out whether this applies to your practice. Three questions, answerable from your own systems:</p></div><div class="mt-10 sm:mt-12"><ol class="surface mt-6 px-5 sm:px-7"><li class="py-7 sm:py-8 "><h3 class="font-serif text-lg leading-snug text-ink">Does your system timestamp technician workup start and end?</h3><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>If no, that is the project, and it comes before any forecasting.</p></div></li><li class="py-7 sm:py-8 border-t border-rule"><h3 class="font-serif text-lg leading-snug text-ink">Pull twelve months of sessions and plot realized technician hours against patient count.</h3><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>If the relationship is tight, a ratio is serving you fine. If the scatter is wide, the width is the size of the problem, in hours, on your own operation.</p></div></li><li class="py-7 sm:py-8 border-t border-rule"><h3 class="font-serif text-lg leading-snug text-ink">Compute what a fixed ratio would have predicted for each of those sessions and how far off it was.</h3><div class="prose-measure mt-2.5 text-[0.9375rem] leading-relaxed text-ink-muted"><p>That is your baseline. Every future claim gets measured against it.</p></div></li></ol><p class="prose-measure mt-4 text-base text-ink-muted">A practice that has done those three things is in a position to evaluate any vendor sceptically, including this one. Kestrel Ridge Health’s<!-- --> <a class="text-primary underline underline-offset-4" href="/pilot/">Phase Zero</a> <!-- -->engagement is structured around exactly that comparison, run on the clinic’s own historical data rather than on synthetic data, and ends with findings transferring to the clinic if the forecast does not beat both the clinic’s current staffing method and a historical average.</p></div></section><section id="sources" class="section-edge rule-top pb-10 pt-16 sm:pb-14 sm:pt-24" data-reveal style="opacity:1;transform:none"><h2 class="max-w-3xl font-serif text-2xl
