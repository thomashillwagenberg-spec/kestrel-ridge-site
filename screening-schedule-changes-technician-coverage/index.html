THE OPERATIONS NOTE / 04 NOV 2026

When screening schedules change, recheck technician coverage

A practical review of the work, skills and coverage behind an approved care-pathway change.

Thomas Wagenberg
Kestrel Ridge Health · 4 minute read

THE PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY

I'd map the affected visits, tasks, sites and half-days before changing technician coverage. Then I'd measure where work actually moves and check competencies, availability and supervision. A lower visit count alone doesn't establish usable staffing capacity.

When I see research suggesting fewer screening visits, my first staffing question is where the remaining work will land. I'd want to know which sessions change, which tasks remain and who needs to be available.

That question comes from the American Academy of Ophthalmology's November 4, 2026 journal update. It highlights FIRST-ROP research on screening timing in a defined group of infants. Today's publication is the Academy's summary. The underlying study appeared earlier in 2026.

What today's update adds to the discussion

The researchers retrospectively compared screening approaches and estimated that a later first examination could reduce visits in the population studied. That result concerns a specific neonatal screening setting. Applying it to another population or changing clinical screening requires the responsible clinicians' assessment.

For an independent ophthalmology practice, I think the broader operational question is worth asking whenever an approved care pathway changes. Does the revised schedule actually release usable technician time, or does it move the same work into different sessions?

Write down what changes before moving coverage

I'd ask the clinical lead to describe the approved change in plain language. Record the effective date, the appointment categories affected and who can resolve exceptions. The operations team needs that boundary before it can build a staffing scenario.

Then I'd map the work around the visit. Preparation, imaging, room turnover, scheduling calls and follow-up administration may sit in different places. A change in exam frequency doesn't tell me which of those tasks changes. I'd have the people doing the work check the list.

Look at each half-day

I'd lay the affected sessions beside the existing plan. Fewer visits across a month could still leave a difficult morning if the remaining appointments cluster there. Check the provider schedule, testing rooms and which qualified people are available at that site.

Record when each task happens. Moving work from the exam room to telephone coordination can change the role carrying it. It also changes which records would capture the time. I'd keep those categories visible so a smaller appointment count doesn't hide the work elsewhere.

Our session workload explanation describes why appointment mix matters. For this review, I'd keep the current schedule and the proposed scenario together so the team can see exactly what assumptions changed.

Measure the effect before treating it as capacity

I'd choose the operational measures before reviewing results. Those might include observed technician minutes per affected session, unfinished testing, overtime and the reasons a scheduler changed coverage. Agree who collects each measure and how missing observations will be handled.

Changes in staffing, appointment mix or documentation during the same period can affect that comparison. I'd record them alongside the results. If the observation period is too short or the measures are incomplete, I'd keep that uncertainty in the decision.

The next question is whether any released time can actually be used. A short gap between appointments may have a different value from a predictable block with a qualified technician available. I'd ask the scheduler what work could fit, then check that against availability and the practice's operating rules.

Where I'd use a workload forecast

At KRH, the proposed workflow separates the estimate of session demand from the rules used to assign people. The forecasting and assignment process keeps a person responsible for the staffing decision.

If your practice is changing a pathway, I'd first check whether its history still describes the future sessions. Mark the change date and review the affected visit categories. Historical averages from the earlier workflow may need a fresh local test.

KRH's published results use synthetic data. We haven't established staffing savings or performance at your clinic. Phase Zero is a fixed-fee diagnostic scoped per clinic, with local evaluation and agreed gates for continuing.

I want the staffing decision to follow the work people can actually observe. A change in visit count gives us a reason to look. The session review gives us something to decide from.
