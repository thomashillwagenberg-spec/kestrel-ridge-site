Planning guide / Independent ophthalmology practices

Before you buy a staffing forecast, check your session data

By Kestrel Ridge Health · Published 2026-11-02 · Updated 2026-11-02

The starting point

Start with a consistent definition of a clinic session, appointment counts by visit type, and a reliable measure of technician work. Then compare a forecast with the planning method your practice already uses on sessions it has not seen. A forecast is useful only if the comparison supports a better planning decision.

A busy afternoon can mean different things to a practice owner and the person assigning technicians. Patient count describes volume. It doesn't tell you how much technician work falls into the same window.

We built this checklist for independently owned ophthalmology practices considering a workload forecast. It's a way to assess what you can measure before buying software. The worksheet is blank. It contains no clinic results, patient records or assumed staffing benchmarks.

1. Agree on what one session means

Choose a consistent planning unit, such as a provider's morning at one site. Write down its boundaries and use the same definition in historical records and future planning. Don't mix a full day at one office with a half-day at another and call them equivalent observations.

Use internal reference codes in your own planning files. A session label should let your team reconcile totals without turning the worksheet into a patient list.

2. Separate volume from technician work

Record appointment counts by the categories your practice actually uses. Note whether counts describe booked or completed visits. Then identify how you measure technician work: observed minutes, documented task duration or another locally defined measure. An estimate and an observation should be labeled differently.

Elapsed appointment time isn't automatically technician time. Neither is a technician's entire paid shift. If those are the only measures available, document that limitation before treating either as a target for a forecast.

4. Give your current method a fair comparison

Write down the existing planning rule before looking at forecast results. Include a simple historical average if it is a plausible alternative. Reserve later sessions for evaluation rather than checking only sessions used to develop the model. Apply the same definitions and time window to each method.

Look beyond an overall average. Check whether the forecast behaves differently by site or session type. Keep a record of missing values and unusual operating days. Don't quietly remove a difficult day because it makes a method look worse.

5. Decide what would make you stop

Agree in advance on the evidence needed to continue. If the available history can't support a reliable comparison, the next step may be improving measurement. If the current spreadsheet works just as well, that is a useful finding too.

KRH's published Phase Zero approach begins with local evaluation and defined continuation gates. Scope and the fixed fee are agreed for the clinic. This guide doesn't quote a price or promise an outcome.

6. Keep workload forecasting separate from assigning people

Even a useful estimate doesn't establish which technician can cover a session. Availability, skills, approved sites and other operating rules still matter. KRH separates the workload model from the assignment rules, with a person making the staffing decision. See how the forecasting and assignment process works.

For any proposed data exchange with KRH, agree on the permitted operational export and handling with the practice's privacy team first. Don't email patient identifiers or a completed operational worksheet through this page.

What this guide does and doesn't establish

The checklist is KRH's proposed planning workflow. It isn't a validated staffing standard. KRH's published model results use synthetic data; they don't demonstrate performance or savings at your practice. Local evaluation is the point of the first step.

For context, read forecasting technician workload by session and the evidence and its limits.
