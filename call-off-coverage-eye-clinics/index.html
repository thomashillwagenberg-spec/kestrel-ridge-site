Call-Off Coverage for Eye Clinics: Options When a Tech Calls Out

When an ophthalmic technician calls out, the decision window is short and the options are limited. This article covers the four real options most clinics have and what each one costs in time, money, and patient experience.

20 October 2026 / 5 minute read Prepared by Kestrel Ridge Health Research Team

Kestrel Ridge Health / Resources / Call-Off Coverage for Eye Clinics

The call-off problem in ophthalmology

A call-off in ophthalmology is not a generic staffing problem. It is a credentialing problem with a short fuse. Ophthalmic technicians hold certifications that reflect 12 to 24 months of supervised clinical training. A general clinical substitute cannot step in. When a credentialed technician calls out, the replacement must also be credentialed, and the pool of available credentialed staff on any given morning is small.

The time pressure compounds the credentialing constraint. Ophthalmic sessions require sequential pre-testing before any patient sees the provider. That pre-testing takes 15 to 30 minutes per patient depending on the session type. If a technician calls out an hour before the session starts, there is not time to find a locum, brief them on practice protocols, and maintain a normal patient flow. The session is already compromised before the first patient arrives.

Patient types add a third constraint. Patients in glaucoma management, retina surveillance, and cataract surgical workup are not easily triaged to a later date without clinical implications. These are monitoring relationships with established baselines. Missing a visit is not a neutral event.

For a broader picture of why same-day staffing is structurally difficult in eye care, see Same-Day Ophthalmology Staffing: What Eye Clinics Need to Know.

The four options

When a technician calls out, most eye clinics have four realistic options. Each has a cost profile that is worth understanding before the call comes in.

Option 1: Absorb with remaining staff

The most common first response is to redistribute the workload among whoever is present. The remaining technicians take on more patients, the session runs slower, and the provider's time is partially wasted waiting for pre-testing to catch up.

The tradeoff is real but absorb-able in some sessions and not in others. A session that was already running at the edge of capacity cannot absorb a one-technician shortfall. A session with built-in buffer can. The problem is that most clinics do not know, in advance, which sessions have buffer and which do not. The answer depends on that specific day's schedule, the mix of procedures, and the specific technicians who are present.

Option 2: Cancel or reduce the patient schedule

When absorption is not feasible, the next option is reducing the day's patient load. This means calling patients before they arrive, which requires administrative time and produces patient dissatisfaction. It also means those patients need to be rescheduled, which adds load to an already constrained schedule in future weeks.

For patients on monitoring protocols, a canceled visit is a clinical event that requires documentation. The provider needs to know which patients were not seen and why. If a patient's condition changes between a canceled visit and a rescheduled one, the canceled visit becomes relevant to the clinical record.

Option 3: Call a locum or agency technician

Some groups maintain relationships with staffing agencies that can place a credentialed ophthalmic technician on short notice. The response time is typically measured in hours, not minutes. Agency rates for same-day placement are materially higher than staff rates. The technician will be credentialed but may be unfamiliar with the practice's EHR, provider preferences, implant protocols, and specific workflow. That unfamiliarity slows the session and increases the likelihood of errors in data entry or patient prep.

Agency coverage is the right answer for some situations. It is expensive and requires advance relationship management to be available when needed. A group that calls an agency for the first time on the morning of a call-out will frequently find the response too slow to be useful.

Option 4: Reassign from another site

Multi-site groups have a fourth option that single-location practices do not: pulling a technician from another site. This works when the other site has capacity to spare, when the technician has the right credentials for the receiving session, and when the travel time between sites allows for same-morning reassignment.

All three conditions must hold simultaneously. In practice, they often do not. The sending site may be running tight itself. The technician may not have the credentials for a specialty session. The sites may be far enough apart that by the time the technician arrives, the session is half over.

Cross-site reassignment also requires someone to be tracking capacity across all sites in real time. Most groups do not have a centralized view of same-day capacity across locations.

How session-level forecasting shifts the odds

None of the four options above becomes easy with forecasting. What changes is the information you have before the call-out occurs.

A session-level forecast, run the evening before, tells you which sessions are most at risk if any single technician is absent. Sessions running near capacity with no credential-matched backup identified are high-risk sessions. Sessions with buffer and a cross-trained technician available are low-risk.

That ranking does not prevent call-outs. It changes the preparation. A group that knows Monday's retina session is the most constrained session of the week can put a standby agreement in place before Monday. A group that finds out Monday morning has no standby in place and is solving from scratch.

The same forecast that identifies high-risk sessions also identifies which technicians at other sites have the credentials to cover each session type. That means when a call-out happens, the first call goes to the right person immediately rather than working through a general on-call list.

For more on how session-level forecasting works, see The Tech Shortage You Cannot Hire Your Way Out Of.

Building a protocol before you need it

The best time to build a call-off coverage protocol is not the morning of a call-out. It is a scheduled review of the sessions most exposed to single-technician call-offs, the cross-credentialing gaps in your current staff, and the agency relationships that need to be established before they are needed.
