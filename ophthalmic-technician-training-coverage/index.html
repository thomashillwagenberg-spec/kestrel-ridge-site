THE OPERATIONS NOTE / 07 NOV 2026

Ophthalmic technician training: plan the coverage first

Put learner time, trainer availability and remaining session coverage on the same plan before approving a training block.

Thomas Wagenberg
Kestrel Ridge Health · 5 minute read

THE PRACTICAL TAKEAWAY

I'd define the learning activity, confirm current task approvals and reserve both learner and trainer time. Then check the remaining session coverage, shared equipment and fallback arrangement before a named person approves the block.

I'd put ophthalmic technician training on the coverage plan before promising the time to someone. The person learning needs room to practise. The person teaching also needs time that hasn't already been assigned to another job.

For an independent ophthalmology practice, I'd start with a small question: who covers the work while this training happens? Writing that down can expose a gap before the session begins. It also gives the scheduler something more useful than a note that says someone is training.

This is my suggested planning process. It isn't a clinical training standard or a claim about results in a KRH customer practice. The clinical lead should define the approved tasks, supervision and sign-off process.

Name the task and the learning arrangement

I'd separate a course, supervised practice and independent coverage. They take different amounts of time and don't establish the same thing. Someone attending a course may be away from patient-facing work. Someone practising a task may need a qualified colleague available beside them.

IJCAHPO describes COA certification as confirming knowledge in specific content areas. Its eligibility routes have different education and work-experience requirements. I'd check the current pathway with IJCAHPO when planning certification support.

I wouldn't use a course booking or an exam date as the date a person automatically becomes independent coverage. I'd ask the clinical lead what the person may do now, what requires supervision and what must happen before that changes.

For the scheduler, the useful output is a clear task and a clear arrangement. Keep detailed learning records in the practice's existing training process. The coverage plan needs the restrictions that affect the session.

Reserve time for the learner and the trainer

I'd look at both schedules together. If the trainer is already covering a testing resource elsewhere, the plan needs a decision. I wouldn't assume that coaching can happen between every other responsibility.

I'd also distinguish presence from availability. Two people can be in the same building while one is committed to another session. A training plan should say when the trainer must be available and who handles their other work during that period.

KRH's published assignment rules include competency, availability and trainee supervision. Its trainee rule prevents a trainee being the only technician assigned to a session. That rule alone doesn't establish that every planned teaching activity has enough protected time.

If a scheduling tool works in half-day blocks, I'd ask how it represents a shorter training activity. An hour reserved for teaching can disappear inside an apparently available morning. Document any manual coverage arrangement instead of assuming the tool has accounted for it.

Use a coverage review before approving the block

I'd take this table to the scheduler and clinical lead. It's a planning checklist, so there are no assumed staffing ratios or invented clinic results. Fill it with the practice's actual requirements before choosing a training time.

I'd check shared rooms and equipment as well as people. Training at a diagnostic station can overlap with the work already booked there. The clinical lead should decide whether the proposed learning arrangement fits that session.

Compare timing options without hiding the trade-off

If the first proposal doesn't fit, I'd compare a different session, a different approved trainer or a different training format. Each option needs its own coverage check. Moving a training block to another site can add travel or remove coverage from the original site.

I wouldn't label an option successful just because the training happened. I'd record whether it happened as planned, whether the agreed supervision was available and whether the schedule needed an override. Those observations can improve the next training block.

If training is cancelled, I'd record a reason and a replacement decision. Otherwise, the calendar may keep showing a learning plan that never happened. A named owner and a new date make the next step visible.

Keep demand, coverage and learning progress separate

A workload forecast estimates expected demand. It doesn't determine whether someone has learned a task. I'd use it to identify sessions that deserve a closer coverage review, then ask the scheduler and clinical lead to approve the arrangement.

For practices considering a local evaluation, I'd ask how training blocks and supervision will be represented before using a forecast in staffing decisions. Our session data readiness guide covers the definitions needed for a useful comparison. Our forecast error review explains how to investigate a miss without confusing demand with the coverage decision.

KRH's governance policy prohibits using its outputs to score individual performance or make employment decisions. I'd keep learning progress in the practice's approved training process. Workload minutes shouldn't become a shortcut for judging a person.

KRH's current evaluation uses synthetic data. It doesn't establish training benefits, savings or staffing results in your clinic. Phase Zero is a diagnostic scoped per clinic, with local evaluation before proceeding.

I want a training commitment to come with a workable coverage arrangement. Name the task, reserve the teaching time and agree what happens if the plan changes. Then the learner, trainer and scheduler are working from the same decision.
