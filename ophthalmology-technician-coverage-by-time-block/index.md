# How to spot a technician coverage gap during an eye clinic session

Use a time-block worksheet to find ophthalmic technician coverage gaps before publishing an eye clinic schedule.

By Thomas Wagenberg · November 13, 2026 · 3 min read

![A wall clock and two empty exam rooms in an ophthalmology clinic.](https://kestrelridgehealth.com/assets/technician-coverage-peaks-20261113.jpg)

*AI-generated editorial illustration of a fictional clinic.*

The practical takeaway

Split the session into short blocks and compare the work due in each block with the qualified technician minutes available. Start with the busiest interval. A morning can have enough minutes overall and still be short when workups and testing land together.

## Start with the work due in each block

I'd take one provider's morning session and mark the half hours where work needs to happen. Count the active minutes for workups, testing and any other duties already assigned. If a task can move, write down its earliest and latest workable times. If it spans two blocks, split the minutes across those blocks.

Then write down the technicians who are present and eligible for each task. Deduct breaks, training and commitments elsewhere. Give each technician one timeline. A person who can do both imaging and workups still has only 30 minutes in a half hour.

## Check the busiest half hour

Here's a fictional 90-minute slice of a morning. Two technicians are scheduled throughout. One is eligible for the tests. The 40 workup minutes at 10:00 could come from four 10-minute workups. The 30 testing minutes could come from two 15-minute tests. The times are example inputs.

| Block | Workups | Tests | Available | Capacity after work |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 9:30 AM | 25 | 15 | 60 | 20 remaining |
| 10:00 AM | 40 | 30 | 60 | 10 short |
| 10:30 AM | 25 | 15 | 60 | 20 remaining |
| Total | 90 | 60 | 180 | 30 remaining |

The slice has 30 minutes left overall. At 10:00, both tests use the full half hour of the eligible technician. That leaves 30 minutes for 40 minutes of workups. I'd move a 10-minute workup to 10:30 only if the visit can move with it and the receiving block still works. The 10:00 block would then have no margin. I'd check what happens if one test runs five minutes long.

## Decide what to change before publishing

1. Try moving flexible work within its allowed window. Recheck both blocks, including downstream room and provider availability.
2. If the work can't move, check whether an eligible technician is free to help. Review the assignment they would leave before moving them.
3. If neither change works, flag the block for the site lead. Adjust the template or coverage before approving it.

A glaucoma-clinic simulation study examined how demands on technicians varied in the clinic it studied. I use that as a reason to look at timing locally. The table here is a planning method with synthetic inputs. It doesn't predict waiting time or establish a safe staffing level.
